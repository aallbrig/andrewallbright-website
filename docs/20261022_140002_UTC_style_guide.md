# Andrew Allbright writing style guide

Derived from reading the 45 published posts on andrewallbright.com (2020 to 2026), mainly the 2023 to 2026 posts:
"Verification Engineering", "Human Touch in an Agentic Codebase", "(Strict) Precommit Hooks: Shifting AI Feedback Left",
"Small List of What I'm Working On", "Embracing AI technology" and "Migrating blog from AWS to Shared Hosting".
The `blog-author` agent follows this guide when drafting.

## Voice

- **First person, conversational, opinionated but humble.** Posts read as "here is what I have noticed and where I landed", not as lectures.
  Hedges such as "I think", "Perhaps", "I'm sure" and "probably" are normal. Strong claims are tied to personal experience.
- **Curious engineer.** The recurring motive is "I get to leverage my desire to learn about things and systems."
  Posts often start from a thing the author noticed or built, then generalize.
- **Light humor and exclamations**, used sparingly: "Giggle.", "Hurray!", "Ta-da", "So fun!", "waterfall, er, I mean spec driven development!"
  Sarcasm is aimed at industry fashions, never at people.
- **Plain words over jargon.** Technical terms are fine for a software audience, but each new idea gets a one-line definition
  (for example: "Verification engineering is the practice of discovering, designing and integrating deterministic feedback...").
- **Responsibility and craft** are recurring values: "we're responsible for the software that's being created on our behalf."
- The reader is a fellow software professional who is curious about agentic tooling, games, or infrastructure.

## Structure

- **Length:** most posts run 150 to 800 words. Long retrospectives and tutorials reach 1,000 to 2,000. Short thought pieces under 200 words are fine.
- **Opening:** one to three sentences that state the observation, trend or question.
  Common openers: "As a software professional I've noticed...", "I've been working with X for a long while now...", "I'm surprised when...".
  When there is a story, tell it first (a hiking trip, the evening a tool was dreamed up).
- **Body:** short paragraphs of 2 to 5 sentences. A rhetorical question is a good pivot ("To what end?", "Aren't the specs...?").
  Narrative arc: naive expectation, reality check, what I do now.
- **Headings:** none in short posts. In list-style posts use `###` per item (project name as the heading).
  Never skip heading levels. The post title is the WordPress title, so the body starts below it with no `#` heading.
- **Closing:** a concrete takeaway, a hint of what is next, or an invitation:
  "Let me know what your thoughts are in the comments below." Avoid recaps that restate the whole post.
- **Lists:** used for tooling and project lists. Do not turn an argument into bullets.

## Mechanics

- American English. Smart quotes are fine in the published HTML, but write straight quotes in the markdown source.
- Use an em dash with spaces around it sparingly, or use commas. Ellipses (...) are used for a trailing thought and at most once per post.
- Project and repo names are written as the project writes them (Treemand, Anthem World, `aallbrig/treemand`).
  Commands, file names and flags go in backticks; multi-line code goes in a fenced block with a language tag.
- Link the first mention of a tool, paper, or repo. Link text is descriptive, never "click here".
- Emoji appear occasionally in titles (a robot for AI posts). Do not add them to body text.
- Titles are short noun phrases or a plain statement: "Verification Engineering", "Migrating blog from AWS to Shared Hosting".
  A parenthetical lead is fine for emphasis: "(Strict) Precommit Hooks: Shifting AI Feedback Left".

## Plain prose

- State a fact or a limit directly: "This applies to the launcher only." Write it that way and skip the framing ("One honest limit.").
- Open a section with its content, with no announcing line ("Here is the whole flow").
- Let a result stand on its own: "It worked, and here is the command."
- Use one aphorism per post at most, and prefer a concrete example.
- Experiences, numbers and opinions come from the author. Ask for them.
- `scripts/test_content.py` holds the list of phrases that read as generic AI prose, and `task ci` fails on them.

## Recurring themes to stay consistent with

- Agentic programming is real and useful, and it needs deterministic feedback (tests, hooks, linters) to be trustworthy.
- Humans stay in the loop and keep real engineering skills (editors, debuggers, tests).
- Games, multiplayer architecture, testing games, and small tools that scratch a personal itch.
- Pragmatism over scale: choose boring hosting, ship the content (see the AWS to shared hosting post).

## Do and don't

| Do | Don't |
|----|-------|
| Start from something I observed or built | Open with a dictionary definition or "In today's fast-paced world" |
| Say what I tried and what happened | Claim certainty I do not have |
| Define a term once, then use it | Stack buzzwords |
| Keep paragraphs short | Write walls of text |
| End with a takeaway or invitation | End with a generic summary |
| Be honest about tradeoffs and failures | Hype tools or sound like marketing copy |
| Prefer concrete numbers and names | Use vague "many" and "various" |

## Short samples of the voice

- "The goal? Never commit "bad" code!"
- "To what end?"
- "I realized building a scalable blog was more important than generating content."

## Markdown rules for posts (enforced by markdownlint)

- Frontmatter has `title`, `slug`, `excerpt`, `categories`, `tags`, `status`, and (after publishing) `wp_post_id`.
- One blank line between blocks. Fenced code always has a language. Images use `![alt text](path)` with real alt text.
- No raw HTML except `<br>`, `<img>`, `<figure>` and `<figcaption>`. Prefer plain markdown so the WordPress conversion stays simple.
